Japan Travel Itinerary

A complete 10-day route through Tokyo, Kyoto, Nara, Osaka and Hakone — with the Shinkansen legs, the things worth booking early, and the timings that decide whether a day works or falls apart.

Why Japan is harder to plan than it looks

Japan rewards planning more than almost anywhere, and punishes the lack of it. The reasons stack up quickly: rail passes that only pay off on certain routes, temples that close earlier than you expect, restaurants that need booking weeks ahead, and cities far enough apart that a badly ordered route costs you a whole day on a train.

Most of the work is not choosing what to see. It is sequencing — grouping sights by neighbourhood so you are not crossing Tokyo twice in a day, and putting the long transfers where they cost you least.

The 10-day route below is the shape most first trips settle into. Treat it as a skeleton to adapt rather than a schedule to follow exactly.

The 10-day Japan itinerary

Days 1–2Tokyo — arrive and acclimatise
Morning
Land at Haneda or Narita, get a transport IC card, drop bags. Don't schedule anything fixed on arrival day.
Afternoon
Shibuya and Harajuku on foot — the crossing, Meiji Shrine, Takeshita Street. Walkable, forgiving of jet lag.
Evening
Shinjuku. Omoide Yokocho and Golden Gai for small bars and yakitori counters.
Note
Day two is the right slot for teamLab — it sells timed tickets that routinely go weeks ahead, so book before you fly.
Days 3–4Tokyo — the quieter half
Morning
Yanaka and Nezu. Low-rise old Tokyo, temple cemeteries, a shopping street that hasn't been modernised.
Afternoon
Asakusa and Senso-ji, then Akihabara for electronics and arcades if that's your thing.
Evening
A department store food hall — the basement floors sell restaurant-quality food, discounted in the last hour.
Note
Keep one of these afternoons unplanned. Tokyo is the city where wandering pays off most.
Day 5Tokyo → Kyoto by Shinkansen
Morning
Travel. The Tokaido Shinkansen runs Tokyo to Kyoto in a little over two hours. Sit on the right-hand side for Mount Fuji on a clear day.
Afternoon
Arrive, drop bags, walk Gion and the Higashiyama lanes before the light goes.
Evening
Pontocho Alley for dinner — narrow, lantern-lit, and lined with everything from cheap to extravagant.
Note
Book a seat rather than riding unreserved if you're travelling with luggage. Oversized bags need a reserved space.
Days 6–7Kyoto — temples, timed properly
Early
Fushimi Inari at first light. The shrine grounds stay open, and arriving before the crowds is the single biggest quality-of-day decision in Kyoto.
Morning
Arashiyama — bamboo grove first, then the temple gardens once the coach parties have moved on.
Afternoon
Nijo Castle and Kinkaku-ji, or the Philosopher's Path if you'd rather walk than queue.
Note
Kyoto rewards slower days. Two temples done properly beat five done at a jog.
Day 8Nara day trip
Morning
Under an hour from Kyoto. Todai-ji and the Great Buddha, then Nara Park.
Afternoon
Kasuga Taisha and its stone lanterns; the wooded paths behind it are usually empty.
Evening
Back to Kyoto for dinner.
Note
The deer are wild animals, not an attraction. They bow for crackers and headbutt when the crackers run out.
Day 9Osaka — eat
Morning
Kuromon Ichiba market. Go hungry; this is breakfast.
Afternoon
Osaka Castle, or Shinsekai for something rougher-edged and less polished.
Evening
Dotonbori. Takoyaki, okonomiyaki, kushikatsu — and the rule about not double-dipping the sauce is real.
Note
Osaka works as a day trip from Kyoto if you'd rather not move hotels. Thirty minutes on the train.
Day 10Hakone, then home
Morning
Hakone for Mount Fuji views, the lake and the ropeway across the volcanic valley.
Afternoon
An onsen ryokan if you have the night — the best possible end to a trip spent walking.
Evening
Back toward Tokyo for your departure, or straight to the airport.
Note
Fuji hides behind cloud more often than not. Treat a clear view as luck, not an itinerary item.

Getting between cities

The Shinkansen does the heavy lifting. Within cities, an IC card on your phone covers metro, bus and most convenience stores, and removes the need to think about fares at all.

Kyoto → NaraLocal / rapid train45m
Kyoto → OsakaLocal / rapid train30m
Osaka → TokyoTokaido Shinkansen2h 30m
Tokyo → HakoneOdakyu line from Shinjuku1h 30m

On the Japan Rail Pass: it is not automatically worth buying. A pass pays off when your route includes several long Shinkansen legs; it rarely does if you are based in one or two cities. Pass pricing rose substantially in recent years, so price your actual route both ways before committing.

Note also that the fastest Tokaido services are not covered by the nationwide pass — check which trains your pass admits you to, not just the route.

How to plan a Japan trip without losing days to it

The order that works: fix the shape first, then the detail. Decide how many nights in each city, book the accommodation and the long-distance legs, and only then fill in what you do each day. Reversing that order is how people end up with a list of forty sights and no route.

  • Book timed-entry attractions before anything else. teamLab, the Ghibli Museum and high-end restaurants sell out weeks ahead. Nothing else in Japan requires that lead time.
  • Group by neighbourhood, not by ranking. A day built around one district beats a day chasing four highlights across the city.
  • Put transfers in the morning. Arriving somewhere at 2pm gives you an afternoon; arriving at 6pm gives you dinner.
  • Leave one day per city unplanned. The days people describe afterwards are almost never the scheduled ones.
  • Check closing days. Many museums close on Mondays, and temple hours shorten considerably in winter.

Before you book anything: verify opening hours, seasonal schedules, ticket availability and local holidays. Golden Week in late April and Obon in mid-August fill trains and hotels nationwide, and prices roughly double.

Common questions

How many days do you need for a Japan trip?

Ten days is the point where Japan stops feeling rushed. It allows roughly four nights in Tokyo, three in Kyoto, and time for Nara, Osaka and one night somewhere slower such as Hakone. Seven days works if you limit yourself to Tokyo and Kyoto. Fourteen lets you add Hiroshima, Kanazawa or the Japanese Alps without moving hotels every night.

Is the Japan Rail Pass worth buying?

It depends on how much long-distance travel you actually do. A pass tends to pay for itself when your route includes several Shinkansen legs, such as Tokyo to Kyoto and back. If you are staying mostly in one or two cities, individual tickets are usually cheaper. Price your specific route both ways before buying — pass pricing has changed significantly in recent years.

Should you start in Tokyo or Kyoto?

Most travellers start in Tokyo, because long-haul flights commonly arrive at Haneda or Narita and jet lag is easier to absorb in a city where you can wander without a fixed schedule. Starting in Kyoto works well if you are flying into Kansai International, and it means you end the trip in Tokyo close to your departure airport.

When is the best time to visit Japan?

Late March to early April brings cherry blossom, and November brings autumn colour — both are beautiful, and both are the most crowded and expensive times to travel. May and early June are mild and far quieter. Summer is hot and humid, and late summer falls within typhoon season. Winter is cold but clear, and it is the cheapest time to go.

Do you need to book restaurants in Japan in advance?

For everyday eating, no — ramen counters, izakayas and department store food halls take walk-ins. Booking matters for high-end sushi, kaiseki dining in Kyoto, and anywhere with a small counter and a reputation. Some of those take reservations only through a hotel concierge or a Japanese phone number, so arrange them before you travel.
